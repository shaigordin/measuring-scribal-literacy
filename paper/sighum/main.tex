\title{Orphans and Archetypes: Quantitative Tradition Mapping\\ for Fragmentary Hittite Texts}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Hittite, the oldest attested Indo-European language, survives on clay tablets from Bronze Age
Anatolia, in modern Turkey. Its archives also record at least eight other languages, above all in rituals
and festivals that the Hittites took over from the peoples around them. These texts are a record of
how the cultures of the region met. Most of them survive only as fragments, and thousands of
fragments cannot be assigned to a composition, because no parallel passage survives. I ask whether
the words on such a fragment can place it in a tradition, and when such a placement can be trusted.
Each manuscript is represented by tf-idf weights of its word forms and sign sequences, counting only
what is visible on the tablet, and compared with the centroids of \Q{trad.countword} traditions
defined in the scholarship; a margin calibrated on held-out fragments of the same size lets the
method abstain, and each decision splits exactly into the share of every word. The method returns
\Q{E1.both.acc} of held-out manuscripts to their tradition and places \Q{E5.assigned} of
\Q{corpus.orphans_used} unassigned fragments at an expected precision of \Q{E2.precision}. Each
placement comes with the words it rests on, which a philologist can check on the tablet. Traditions
are recognised by whom and what a text names, and hardly by how its scribe spelled it. Code, data
and results are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Hittite is the oldest attested Indo-European language. It was written in cuneiform in central
Anatolia, in modern Turkey, from the 16th to the early 12th century BCE. Most texts come from the
archives of the capital Ḫattuša (modern Boğazköy); smaller archives were found at Šapinuwa,
Šamuḫa, Tapikka and Šarišša \citep{bryce2005kingdom,vandenhout2020literacy}. These archives are
multilingual. Besides Hittite they hold texts in Luwian, Palaic, Hattian, Hurrian, Akkadian and
Sumerian, and words of an Indo-Aryan language \citep{vandenhout2020literacy}; a ninth language,
named after the town of Kalašma, was published only recently \citep{rieken2024kalasma}. Much of this variety is found in rituals and
festivals. The Hittites collected them from Kizzuwatna in the southeast, from Arzawa in the west
and from northern Syria, and each tradition kept its own gods, specialists and ritual language.
Together they show how the cultures of the region met in cult.

These texts reach us broken. Some 30,000 tablets and fragments survive, most of them small pieces.
For more than a century editors have fitted them together through joins, duplicates and parallel
passages: a puzzle without the picture on the box. TLHdig, the digital edition of the
Hethitologie-Portal Mainz, now makes the corpus machine-readable \citep{tlhdig04}. Its release 0.4
holds \Q{tlh.texts} manuscripts with about \Q{tlh.words} million words.

Laroche's \textit{Catalogue des textes hittites} \citep{laroche1971catalogue}, kept up to date
online \citep{cthportal}, gave every composition a number and with it a place: rituals in one
block, festivals in another, Hattian, Luwian and Hurrian texts in blocks of their own. Fragments
that could not be placed went into residual groups. The largest is \CTH{470}, ``fragments of
rituals'', with \Q{orph.cth470_texts} manuscripts, and TLHdig adds \Q{orph.ebo_texts} small EBo
fragments filed under two more residual numbers. I call these unassigned fragments
\term{orphans}. Figure~\ref{fig:fragment} shows one of them.

A philologist places a fragment through a parallel: a duplicate, or a close passage in a known
composition. Most orphans have none. Computation offers another route. Simply put, it treats
placement as attribution: can the words on a fragment point to its tradition? Attribution has two
known weak points. It becomes less reliable the shorter the text is \citep{eder2015size}, and a
method that must choose among fixed candidates always chooses one, even when the right answer is
not among them \citep{koppel2011wild}. A placement is also useful only if a philologist can check
it.

\citet{gordin2025matters} mapped the festival texts without assigning them. Here I build a
placement method that meets these three demands. It is tested on manuscripts whose tradition is
known, it says ``undecided'' when the evidence is too thin, and it shows the words each decision
rests on. My contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item an open-set placement method for very short, damaged texts. It counts only what is
    visible on the tablet and abstains below a margin calibrated on fragments of the same size
    (Section~\ref{sec:method});
  \item an exact decomposition of each decision over its words, which turns a placement into an
    argument a philologist can check (Section~\ref{sec:decision});
  \item placements for \Q{E5.assigned} orphans, each with its diagnostic words and nearest
    manuscripts (Section~\ref{sec:orphans});
  \item evidence that traditions are recognised by what a text names, not by how it is spelled,
    and a test of scholarly groupings that holds out whole compositions
    (Sections~\ref{sec:recovery}--\ref{sec:decision}).
\end{enumerate}
In what follows, I first describe the traditions, the data and the method. I then test the method,
show what its decisions rest on, and place the orphans.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Traditions and the catalogue.}
Laroche sorted by genre first and by language second. Later scholarship defines a ritual tradition
by the origin of its practitioners, its cult and its ritual language, so one tradition can be
scattered over several CTH ranges. The ritual of the Kizzuwatnean Old Woman Maštigga, for
instance, is filed with the named practitioners at \CTH{404}, not with the Kizzuwatna rituals at
\CTH{471}--500 \citep{miller2004studies}. The rituals of the AZU priest (\CTH{701}) sit in the
Hurrian block \citep{salvini1986chs}, and the named-practitioner rituals of \CTH{390}--469 come
from Arzawa, the Lower Land and Kizzuwatna alike
\citep{christiansen2006ambazzi,bawanypeck2005auguren}. The festivals have been regrouped by cult
geography \citep{gorke2022hurrian,trameri2024kizzuwatna} and, in the Hethitische Festrituale
project (HFR), into edition modules such as the Luwian and the Palaic festivals
\citep{hfr,gorke2020hatti}.

\paragraph{Clusters of the festival texts.}
\citet{gordin2025matters} measured the tf-idf similarity of about 1,000 Hurrian and Luwian
festival manuscripts and found three clusters \citep[109f.]{gordin2025matters}. To compare them with
the scholarship, the study summarised earlier attributions as four geographic and cultural
spheres: two Hurrian traditions (Kizzuwatna--Lower Land and Syro-Mesopotamian), the Luwian cult
centres, and a group with mixed cultic elements \citep[Table~3]{gordin2025matters}. The Luwian
festivals formed one cluster; the two Hurrian groups were split. The study read the large shared
vocabulary as a trace of copying and editing in the scriptoria of Ḫattuša
\citep[112--115]{gordin2025matters}. It called its grouping schematic. I therefore treat the four
spheres as hypotheses and test them (Section~\ref{sec:recovery}).

\paragraph{Attribution and cuneiform.}
Hittite scribes copied and re-edited ritual texts over two centuries, and their spelling habits
vary with date, school and individual
\citep{gordin2015scribal,weeden2011logograms,vandenhout2009dating,waal2015diplomatics}.
Stylometry supplies frequency-based representations, distance measures and held-out evaluation
\citep{burrows2002delta,juola2008authorship,stamatatos2009survey}; open-set recognition supplies
ways to reject a sample that fits no candidate \citep{scheirer2013openset}. Machine learning on
cuneiform has addressed transliteration and translation \citep{gordin2020reading,yavasan2025clay},
language identification \citep{jauhiainen2019cuneiform}, matching fragments to known compositions
by sign sequences \citep{simonjetz2024reconstruction}, and palaeography \citep{yugay2024stylistic}.
Fragment matching finds only a known text, and all these studies choose among fixed candidates. I
know of no study that places Hittite fragments in traditions by their content, or that asks when a
fragment fits none of them.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Corpus and annotation.}
I use TLHdig 0.4 \citep{tlhdig04}, one row per word as in \citet{yavasan2025clay}. TLHdig has three
layers. The \term{transliteration} is the editors' reading. It marks the state of each sign
(preserved, damaged, restored, lost), logograms and determinatives, and gives a normalised form of
each word \citep[8]{hfrannotation}. The \term{cuneiform} layer renders each line in Unicode,
without word division. The \term{analysis}, made by the HFR tool \citep{hfr}, gives each word a
lemma, a German dictionary gloss and a form. Most analyses are automatic. Some files are
\textit{pre-validated}: an annotator inspected the automatic analyses and selected one
\citep[14]{hfrbasiscorpus}. These are most festival texts but almost no rituals or orphans.
Placement reads only the transliteration. The analysis serves the word categories
(Section~\ref{sec:method}) and decides which words have a normalised form. Where it is missing, as
for the Luwian rituals, a word takes the lemma TLHdig gives the same spelling elsewhere, if all
occurrences agree, or the single lemma eDiAna \citep{ediana} gives a Luwian spelling. Every such
transfer keeps its source passage. Reference manuscripts need at least 17 legible words
\citep{gordin2025matters}.

\paragraph{Damage and restoration.}
TLHdig prints the editors' restorations next to what survives. Counting them would let an editor's
reconstruction, often made from a duplicate, decide where a fragment belongs. HFR's critical
editions, too, keep the composite text apart from each tablet's transliteration
\citep[6, 12]{hfrkritisch}. I therefore count only what is visible on the tablet. A word counts if
at least half its signs survive, or if the same word survives at least half-preserved elsewhere in
the same text. Sign sequences never span a missing sign. This text (\term{without restorations})
is used throughout; the editors' full text (\term{with restorations}) serves as a comparison. Both
are released.

\paragraph{Orphans.}
Orphans are all \CTH{470} manuscripts and all EBo manuscripts, \Q{orph.orphans_total} in all. Of
those in the CTH ranges I load, \Q{corpus.orphans_used} have at least five legible words and are
placed; their median is \Q{corpus.orphan_median} words. Orphans never enter the reference.

\paragraph{Reference traditions.}
The \Q{trad.countword} traditions come from the studies that argued for them
(Table~\ref{tab:traditions}). The Hurrian festival groups follow \citet[Table~3]{gordin2025matters}.
Its Luwian group is split as HFR splits it, and HFR's Palaic festivals are added \citep{hfr}. The
ritual traditions are the Kizzuwatna rituals as delimited by \citet{miller2004studies}; the
western rituals, that is, the augur rituals \citep{bawanypeck2005auguren} and those whose colophon
names Arzawa or Ḫapalla; the Luwian rituals of Puriyanni and Kuwattalla \citep{yakubovich2023luwili};
and the Hattian cult layer of \citet{klinger1996hattische}. The Ambazzi rituals show Luwian
influence but no stated link to Arzawa \citep{christiansen2006ambazzi}. They stay outside the
reference and are tested separately (Section~\ref{sec:recovery}). Two more sets are placed like
orphans: the rituals of \CTH{390}--469 that no study claims, and \CTH{500}, a running list of
Kizzuwatna fragments. For \CTH{701}, the subgroups of \citet{salvini1986chs} serve as partial
ground truth.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

The method has four steps: represent, compare, decide or abstain, and explain.
Figure~\ref{fig:case} follows one orphan, \C{txtid}, through all four with its actual values.

\paragraph{Representing a text.}
Every manuscript is represented in two ways \citep{salton1988weighting}. The word space holds
tf-idf weights of normalised word forms; it stands for \term{what} is written. The sign space holds
tf-idf weights of sign sequences of one to three signs within a word; it stands for \term{how} it is
written. The main model joins both. Weights are sublinear, each row has unit length, and features
found in fewer than two manuscripts are dropped.

\paragraph{Comparing and deciding.}
Each tradition is represented by the normalised centroid of its manuscripts. A fragment is
compared with every centroid by cosine similarity. It is placed in the nearest tradition only if
its lead over the runner-up, the \term{margin}, is large enough. I set this threshold on held-out
data, not by hand. Reference manuscripts are cut into random windows of 3 to 40 words, as short as
the orphans, and the threshold is the smallest margin at which these windows reach
\Q{E2.precision} precision. \C{txtid} is nearest to the \C{topshort} and well above the threshold,
so it is placed. The words that decide are the logograms of the AZU priest, the offering and the
table, and \C{nn.mode} of its five nearest manuscripts belong to his rituals
(Figure~\ref{fig:case}, panel 6). To my knowledge no study has assigned this fragment
\citep[46f.]{roszkowska2007kbo44}, so the link is a new proposal for the philologist to test.

\paragraph{Explaining a decision.}
Because cosine similarity of unit vectors is a dot product, the margin splits exactly into one term
per feature, $x_f(c_{\mathrm{top},f}-c_{2,f})$, where $x$ is the fragment and $c$ the two
centroids. Summing these terms by word category shows what a placement rests on. Listing the
largest gives the words a philologist can check. Simply put, the method does not only say where a
fragment belongs; it says why.

\paragraph{Validation.}
Each reference manuscript is removed from its own centroid and placed again
(leave-one-manuscript-out). I compare accuracy with two baselines: always guessing the largest
tradition, and the same method with shuffled labels (200 permutations, which also give a
$p$-value). A stricter test removes whole compositions (Section~\ref{sec:recovery}).

\paragraph{Why only what is visible.}
The orphan KBo 57.54 shows why restorations matter. With them it is placed with the Luwian rituals.
The deciding word is the Old Woman, \textsuperscript{MUNUS}ŠU.GI, a key title in those rituals,
restored both times it appears. Without restorations its lead falls below the threshold, and it
stays unplaced. This is not a single case. Of the \Q{AC.onlyA} orphans placed only with
restorations, \Q{AC.onlyA.luwian} go to the Luwian rituals, and \Q{AC.onlyA.luwian.sugi} of these
rest on a restored \textsuperscript{MUNUS}ŠU.GI. Counting restorations would let the editors'
expectations place the fragments.

\paragraph{Word categories.}
Word categories play no part in placement. They are added afterwards to ask what a decision rests
on. I use the categories of \citet[Table~4]{gordin2025matters}: agents, deities, function words,
foreign words, realia and verbs. Other nouns are grouped by meaning (places, nature, abstract
nouns, adjectives, measures, numbers), and colophon formulas are set aside like function words. No
word was classed by hand. Fixed rules use the determinative, the language tag and the
morphological analysis \citep[13]{hfrbasiscorpus}. Nouns are then classed by the German gloss of
their lemma in HFR's lexicon \citep[6f.]{hfrannotation}: a dictionary gloss, not a translation of
the passage. Labels are thus projected from a lexicon, unlike projection through aligned
translations for Greek and Latin \citep{yousef2022alignment,yousef2023projection}. The mapping of
glosses to classes was drafted with a language model and checked in three ways: against the Open
German WordNet \citep{siegel2021odenet}, where disagreements went to an expert; by
\Q{sense.rules} context rules for glosses with several senses, each checked in the dictionaries
\citep{chd,cad,cda,tischler2001hhw,tischler1977heg}; and by a stratified audit of \Q{wc.audit.n}
words, which found \Q{wc.audit.after.corpus} of all labels correct. The list and the rules are
released for correction.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Validation and fragment size}
\label{sec:validation}
The method returns most manuscripts to their tradition. Held out one at a time, \Q{E1.both.acc} of
the reference manuscripts are placed correctly, against \Q{E1.both.majority} for the largest
tradition and \Q{E1.both.null_mean} for shuffled labels. Recall ranges from \Q{trad.minrecall} to
\Q{trad.maxrecall} (Table~\ref{tab:summary}); it is lowest for the Syro-Mesopotamian and the mixed
Hurrian groups. Counting restorations changes little (\Q{A.E1.both.acc}).

\subsection{Testing scholarly groupings}
\label{sec:recovery}
Holding out single manuscripts flatters the method, because the other copies of the same
composition stay in the reference. A stricter test removes a whole composition, rebuilds the
centroids, and asks whether the composition returns to the group scholarship assigned it to. This
turns the method into a test of the groupings themselves. \Q{E10a.compositions_correct} of
\Q{E10a.compositions} compositions return (Table~\ref{tab:summary}). The Hattian cult layer, the
Ḫuwaššanna festivals, the Luwian rituals and the Kizzuwatna rituals are recovered as groups. The
Hurrian festival groups and the Palaic festivals are not. Their manuscripts resemble each other
because they share compositions, not because their compositions share a profile of their own. The
rituals of the AZU priest, held out whole, go to the Kizzuwatna rituals: in content, they are
Kizzuwatna rituals. I read a group that is not recovered as a claim these features do not support,
not as one the evidence refutes.

\paragraph{A contested attribution.}
The Ambazzi rituals (\CTH{\Q{E10b.cths}}) have been read as western because of their Luwian
elements \citep{christiansen2006ambazzi}. Scored as a group against every tradition, and against
random sets of unclaimed rituals of the same size, they come second closest to the western
rituals ($p\Q{E10b.claim.p}$). The Kizzuwatna rituals remain nearer. The western reading is
plausible, but not proven.

\paragraph{Robustness.}
Two variants confirm the main result. On spellings alone, with every manuscript of 17 or more
legible words, accuracy falls only slightly, because the added manuscripts are harder to place
(Appendix~\ref{sec:spellings}). On TLHdig's Unicode cuneiform, without the editors' readings or word
division, accuracy is \Q{UA.acc}, and all orphans placed by both versions go to the same tradition
(Appendix~\ref{sec:unicode}).

\subsection{What a placement rests on}
\label{sec:decision}
Traditions differ in whom and what they name, not in how they spell. Every layer alone identifies a
tradition well above chance (Table~\ref{tab:layers}); foreign-language words do best. The decisive
test concerns the words attested in more than one spelling. If spelling carried tradition beyond
the word itself, keeping their spelling would beat collapsing it to the normalised form. It does
not (\Q{E7.spelling.acc} against \Q{E7.collapsed.acc}). This is the line HFR's editions also draw
between variants and mere spellings \citep[10f.]{hfrkritisch}. By word category, removing the
function words changes nothing, while removing the foreign words or the agents costs most.

\paragraph{Similarity is not decision.}
The decomposition answers an obvious worry. The particle \textit{nu} and other frequent words weigh
heavily in how similar a fragment is to every tradition. But they weigh little in the decision
between two. Features found in at least a quarter of the reference make up \Q{E11b.common.similarity}
of the similarity but only \Q{E11b.common.ref} of the decision, because every centroid shares them.
The decision rests above all on agents and deities (\Q{E11a.decision.agentsanddeities}), then on
realia and foreign words. Each tradition has its own decisive category (Table~\ref{tab:summary}):
realia for the Kizzuwatna rituals, agents and deities for the festivals and the Hattian cult layer.

Common words matter for short orphans in another way. They rarely send a fragment to the wrong
tradition, but they often supply the margin that lets it be placed at all. I therefore release the
decomposition with every placement and treat a placement that rests mostly on common words as weak.

\subsection{Orphans and the open set}
\label{sec:orphans}
The method places \Q{E5.assigned} of the \Q{corpus.orphans_used} orphans, \Q{E5.top.share} of them
with the \Q{E5.top}. The two test sets show how to read these placements. The unclaimed rituals of
\CTH{390}--469 go mostly to the Kizzuwatna rituals, the only large ritual class (\Q{E5b.res.kizz}
of \Q{E5b.res.assigned}). Here genre comes before origin. \CTH{500}, by contrast, goes mostly to
the Hurrian festival groups, in line with HFR's treatment of it as festival material \citep{hfr}.

A fixed set of traditions always names one \citep{koppel2011wild}. Windows from \Q{E9.genres.count}
other genres, such as myths and oracles, still pass the margin in \Q{E9.genres.placed.lo}--\Q{E9.genres.placed.hi}
of cases. Adding these genres as classes of their own catches most of them. Among the orphans, it
finds \Q{E9.orph.outside} that are closer to another genre, and I release them as candidates for
re-filing.

Counting restorations would place far more orphans (\Q{A.E5.assigned}). But the placements kept
without restorations are stable: \Q{AC.placedA.samenearest.share} of the orphans placed with
restorations keep the same nearest tradition without them. The loss is in confidence, not in
direction.

\subsection{Duplicates, not joins}
\label{sec:joins}
The same similarities find duplicates. For \Q{KJ.duplicate.pairs} duplicate pairs recorded in the
Konkordanz, the partner's median rank is \Q{KJ.duplicate.median}. Joins are different: two pieces
of one tablet share context but rarely text (median rank \Q{KJ.join.median}). A ranking model
trained on known joins narrows the search, but does not tell a join from a duplicate
(Appendix~\ref{sec:joinmodel}). That needs physical evidence.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Genre before origin.}
A placement means ``nearest of these traditions''. The largest layer a fragment shares with the
reference is its genre, as the unclaimed rituals show. A placement also does not return a fragment
to a CTH number. The catalogue is a modern construct, and many manuscripts fit more than one of
its groups. Extending the method to the whole corpus therefore needs genre and composition
categories from the scholarship, not CTH numbers.

\paragraph{Archetypes and witnesses.}
The results suggest a division of labour between two levels of a text. I call the method built on
it Quantitative Tradition Mapping (QTM). In the terms of textual criticism
\citep{maas1958textual,trovato2014lachmann}, a tradition is an \term{archetype}. It is not a
reconstructed text, but the vocabulary, formulae and foreign-language recitation its manuscripts
share. Each manuscript or orphan is a \term{witness} to part of it. Hittitology has rarely aimed at
an \textit{Urtext}; its editions combine the best-preserved manuscripts
\citep[39f.]{christiansen2011methoden}, and variants could arise from memorisation as well as copying
\citep{marcuson2016memorization}. In my results the archetype is recognised by content, above all
by whom and what a text names. Spelling marks the witness, as the habits of scribal circles would
\citep{gordin2015scribal}.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
Computation cannot read a broken tablet for the philologist. It can, however, do three things a
reader of one fragment cannot. It compares a fragment with every manuscript of every tradition at
once. It says, with a measured error, when the evidence is too thin. And it shows the words a
placement rests on. For Hittite, this turns thousands of orphans into hypotheses that can be
checked. From about ten legible words, the words that name gods, specialists and objects point to
the tradition a fragment came from, and with it to the region whose cult it records.

\section*{Limitations}
The study rests on one digital edition, whose analyses are mostly automatic, and on lemmas
transferred from the same spelling, which assumes one lemma per spelling. Several traditions are
small (the smallest has \Q{trad.smallest.n} manuscripts). The traditions come from the literature:
the method tests them but cannot find a new one. The meaning classes rest on a model-drafted gloss
mapping, checked against WordNet and on a sample, not in full. Every placement is a hypothesis to
verify on the tablet.

\section{The cuneiform layer}
\label{sec:unicode}
I repeat the evaluation on TLHdig's Unicode cuneiform, which gives the signs without the
editors' readings (analysed manuscripts only). Without word division, each line is a sequence of
signs, cut at broken signs, and I count sign 1--3-grams: accuracy is \Q{UA.acc}, against
\Q{UA.translit.acc} for the transliteration on the same reference; \Q{UA.placed} of
\Q{UA.scored} orphans are placed (\Q{UA.translit.placed} from the transliteration), and all
\Q{UA.both} placed by both go to the same tradition. With word division, carried over from the
transliteration where a line's sign count matches (\Q{U.align} of lines), accuracy falls to
\Q{UB.acc}; more orphans are placed (\Q{UB.placed}), and of the \Q{UB.both} placed by both,
\Q{UB.same} agree. The readings thus add little to placement, while word division that cannot be
aligned adds noise. The Unicode strings include restored signs, and the placement margin was set
on the transliteration. KBo 44.52 goes to the \Q{UA.kbo4452} tradition in both runs.


\section{A ranking model for joins}
\label{sec:joinmodel}
TLHdig marks on every line of a joined text the piece it is preserved on. Splitting its joined
texts back into their pieces gives \Q{J1.query_pieces} query pieces from \Q{J1.joined_texts}
joins, each to be found among \Q{J1.units} units. A logistic regression re-ranks the nearest
candidates of each piece over twelve pair features of text, spelling, preserved sides and
columns, publication and excavation series, and size.
It is tested on compositions it was not fitted on (five folds grouped by CTH number). The gain is
real but modest (Table~\ref{tab:joins}): the partner reaches the top 20 for \Q{J1.model.r20} of
pieces, against \Q{J1.sim.r20} for similarity alone, and the median rank falls from
\Q{J1.sim.median} to \Q{J1.model.median}. On the Konkordanz's suspected joins the model places
\Q{J1.konk.join.model.hits20} of \Q{J1.konk.join.queries} in the top 20, against
\Q{J1.konk.join.sim.hits20} for similarity.

In its weights, word overlap (\Q{J1.w.wordoverlap}) and similarity
(\Q{J1.w.cosine}) favour a join, as do preserved columns that match (\Q{J1.w.columnoverlap}),
identical spellings (\Q{J1.w.samespelling}) and the same excavation series
(\Q{J1.w.sameseries}); the scribal rates have no measurable effect (\Q{J1.w.scribaldistance},
95\% interval \Q{J1.w.scribaldistance.ci}). Shared text is the strongest cue, so the model still
rewards what duplicates have in common. Find spot and
date, the evidence one would want, cannot be fitted honestly, because for known joins the
Konkordanz often derives them from the join itself. Text alone narrows the search
for a join but does not tell a join from a duplicate; that needs physical evidence.
